MINIMAX H3 PROMPTING FOR AI FILMMAKERS
Practical T2VA, I2VA, FL2VA, and REF2VA patterns, with audio and continuity tips

Version: August 2026


1. WHAT MAKES AN H3 PROMPT DIFFERENT

MiniMax H3 is best treated as an audiovisual timeline generator, not as an image model
that happens to output several frames. A useful prompt describes what can be seen and
heard as time advances. It should establish the subject, action, camera, environment,
sound, and the intended relationship to any supplied image, video, or audio reference.

The most reliable prompts are concrete and chronological. They explain observable
changes instead of using abstract goals such as "make it cinematic" or "keep it
consistent." Cinematic adjectives can still be useful, but they should support clear
instructions about composition, motion, light, editing, dialogue, and sound.

Use the correct task family:

- T2VA: text creates the complete video and audio timeline.
- I2VA: one image defines the target at the beginning; H3 develops motion from it.
- FL2VA or FFLF: two images define the first and last states; H3 creates a continuous
  path between them.
- L2VA: a last-frame image defines where the generated action must finish.
- REF2VA: images, video, and audio can define subjects, appearance, motion, editing,
  voice, sound, or keyframes without necessarily becoming the first frame.

Do not confuse a subject reference with a keyframe. A subject reference says who or
what should remain recognizable. A picture used as a keyframe also constrains framing,
pose, environment, and placement at a particular time.


2. THE CORE PROMPT DESIGN METHOD

Build the prompt in five passes.

Pass 1 - State the timeline contract

For image-conditioned work, state where each key image belongs on the target timeline.
In I2VA, the first image belongs at 0.00 seconds. In FL2VA, state that Picture 1 is the
opening state and Picture 2 is the final state, including the exact final timestamp.
For a 243-frame output at 24 fps, the visible timeline is 10.125 seconds, normally
written as 10.13 seconds.

Pass 2 - Describe the shot as observable events

Write what changes, in order. Include actions, reactions, object interactions,
environmental changes, and the destination state. Avoid repeatedly redescribing the
starting image. In FL2VA, the intermediate transformation is the main creative task.

Pass 3 - Direct the camera

Name the move, its speed, and its magnitude when those details matter. Useful terms
include slow push-in, rapid pull-back, left pan, right truck, pedestal down, handheld
tracking, smooth arc, static locked shot, controlled shake, POV, and roll. Also state
whether the camera follows the subject or lets the subject move inside a fixed frame.

Pass 4 - Design the sound

Separate three things:

1. Dialogue or narration, attached to the correct speaker.
2. Diegetic ambience and effects that belong inside the scene.
3. Non-diegetic music added for the audience.

This prevents dialogue, ambience, and music from competing or being described twice.

Pass 5 - Remove contradictions

Check that the requested action fits the duration, camera instructions do not conflict,
speaker identities remain stable, and reference roles are not ambiguous. Ten seconds
usually supports one developed shot, two simple shots, or three very short beats. It is
rarely enough for several complex actions plus multiple camera changes and long speech.


3. MODE-SPECIFIC STRUCTURES

3.1 T2VA

Recommended structure:

integrated_multimodal_description:
[Shot 1] Subject, setting, action, camera, lighting, and any dialogue.
[Shot 2] At 00:SS.mmm, describe an explicit cut only if one is really needed.

overall_soundscape:
Ambient sound, synchronized physical effects, room tone, and non-verbal vocal sounds.

non_diegetic_music:
Instrumentation, tempo, pulse, energy curve, and ending; or N/A.

T2VA example - 10-second landscape shot

integrated_multimodal_description:
[Shot 1] At blue hour, a lone bicycle courier in a yellow raincoat waits beneath a
flickering tram-stop sign on a rain-polished street. The camera begins in a wide static
composition, then performs a very slow push-in as an empty tram glides behind him. He
checks the handwritten address on a damp envelope, hears a distant bell, and looks up
with sudden recognition. Reflections stretch across the road while the push-in settles
on a medium close-up. He does not speak. Natural wet-night contrast, realistic motion,
restrained expressions, no cut.

overall_soundscape:
Steady light rain, soft tire hiss on wet asphalt, tram motor and rail vibration passing
behind the courier, one distant bicycle bell, subtle coat movement, continuous city room
tone. No dialogue.

non_diegetic_music:
Sparse low piano notes and a quiet sustained cello texture, approximately 62 BPM, slowly
building until the courier looks up, then ending without a dramatic hit.


3.2 I2VA

Start by making the time alignment unambiguous: Picture 1 is the complete visual state
at 0.00 seconds. Then describe how motion grows out of that state. Do not ask the model
to recreate a different opening composition.

I2VA example

Picture 1 defines the full target frame at 0.00 seconds.

integrated_multimodal_description:
[Shot 1] Continue naturally from Picture 1. The woman initially remains still while a
light breeze moves only the loose strands of her hair and the linen curtain behind her.
She slowly turns toward the open window, lifts the cup with her right hand, and smiles
at something outside the frame. The camera makes a restrained six-second arc to the
right, preserving her identity, clothing, window placement, and warm morning light.
During the final seconds the camera becomes still and she takes one small sip. One
continuous shot, realistic hand motion, no sudden zoom.

overall_soundscape:
Quiet apartment room tone, curtain fabric, distant morning traffic, porcelain cup
touching the saucer, one soft breath.

non_diegetic_music:
N/A.


3.3 FL2VA / FFLF

FL2VA works best as a single continuous shot. First establish Picture 1 at 0.00 seconds
and Picture 2 at the final target time. Then describe the physical and camera path that
connects them. The model needs the middle of the action, not another description of the
two endpoints.

FL2VA example - 243 frames at 24 fps

Picture 1 is the complete opening state at 0.00 seconds. Picture 2 is the complete final
state at 10.13 seconds.

integrated_multimodal_description:
[Shot 1] Beginning exactly from Picture 1, the child releases the red paper boat into
the narrow stream and walks beside it. The camera tracks smoothly at the child's waist
height, moving forward and slightly left while keeping the boat in the lower foreground.
Water speed increases gradually; the child takes three quick steps and reaches toward
the boat without touching it. Between seconds seven and nine the tracking move curves
around the child, allowing the stone bridge and evening lanterns to enter their final
positions. Motion decelerates during the last second and resolves exactly into Picture
2, preserving the child's face, coat, boat color, weather, and direction of travel. One
continuous shot, no cut, no teleportation, no sudden change of lens.

overall_soundscape:
Flowing shallow water, small paper splashes, quick footsteps on damp stones, quiet wind
under the bridge, distant evening birds.

non_diegetic_music:
Gentle pizzicato strings with a light wooden flute, playful but understated, slowing and
resolving as the final frame is reached.

FL2VA troubleshooting:

- If the ending arrives too early, reduce the number of actions and describe a gradual
  transition with a final one-second settling phase.
- If identity changes, emphasize the invariant identity and clothing once, then simplify
  the action. Repeating the same identity sentence in every beat usually adds noise.
- If the model cuts between images, explicitly request one continuous shot and describe
  a camera path that can physically connect both compositions.
- If the endpoints are too different to connect, insert an intermediate generated
  keyframe and run two simpler chunks instead of forcing an impossible transformation.


3.4 L2VA

L2VA asks H3 to invent a plausible earlier state and converge on the supplied final
image. Describe the destination timing and the approach to it. Avoid overconstraining
the unseen beginning.

L2VA example

Picture 1 defines the complete target frame at 10.13 seconds.

integrated_multimodal_description:
[Shot 1] A street magician completes a card flourish while the camera performs a slow
handheld approach through a small audience. Loose cards rotate down through the warm
stage light. The magician catches the final card, turns it toward the camera, and the
camera movement progressively stabilizes. During the last two seconds the crowd, hand,
card, and camera settle naturally into the exact composition of Picture 1. One shot.

overall_soundscape:
Small crowd reactions, card flicks, fabric movement, distant pedestrian ambience, a
brief natural cheer as the final card is revealed.

non_diegetic_music:
N/A.


4. FULL-REFERENCE (REF2VA) PROMPTING

Full-reference output is a production specification with six mandatory parts. Write the
six headings in English and keep this sequence:

subject_definitions
summary
retention_analysis
detailed_description
overall_soundscape
non_diegetic_music

English is also the working language inside these sections. Keep another language only
when it belongs to an exact spoken or sung line inside <d>, or to lettering that must be
visible in the generated scene. This makes the control language consistent without
silently translating performance text or signage.


4.1 BUILD A REFERENCE REGISTRY BEFORE WRITING THE VIDEO

The labels form a registry shared by all six sections. Once a label has been assigned,
its meaning must not drift later in the prompt. Numbers follow connection order inside
each media category, not the order in which the labels happen to be mentioned.

<Subject N>

Use this for visible content that H3 must recognize and reuse: a person, creature,
vehicle, prop, costume, room, landscape, interface, visual effect, pose, expression, or
movement signature. The subject is the reusable concept extracted from an asset, not
the source file itself. One subject can combine evidence from several inputs, and one
input can define several distinct subjects.

Example:

<Subject 1> is the rescue pilot whose face and orange flight suit come from <Picture 1>,
while the controlled limp in her walk is guided by <Video 1>.

<Picture N>

Create a standalone picture label only when the image itself controls a concrete target
frame or shot plan: an opening frame, final frame, intermediate keyframe, composition
anchor, or storyboard panel. When an image supplies only identity, clothing, setting, or
style, cite it inside the appropriate Subject definition and do not give the file a
second independent role.

Example of a genuine frame anchor:

<Picture 2> is the final-frame target for [Shot 2], fixing the subject positions, camera
height, and arrangement of the rescue lights.

<Video N>

Use this label for a relationship with the source video as a timeline: direct editing,
continuation after its ending, preservation of its cut pattern, or borrowing its camera
and temporal design. A character found inside that video is still defined as a Subject.
The Video label tracks the source sequence; it does not replace the character label.

<Audio N>

Use this for an enabled standalone audio source or an explicitly selected audio track
from a reference video. It may supply the final signal, a selected passage, a voice,
lyrics, beat, ambience, effects, or a general sonic character. A video that happens to
contain sound does not automatically create an Audio label. The audio must have a real
job in the requested result.

Picture, Video, and Audio counters are independent. For example, <Video 1> and <Audio 2>
may originate from the same uploaded file. Their unequal numbers do not imply unrelated
sources. Mention their shared provenance only when the relationship would otherwise be
unclear.

If an Audio reference controls a visible speaker's voice, bind it to that already
planned speaker:

<Audio 1> is a vocal-character reference for <Subject 2> (S1), guiding pitch, grain,
breath spacing, and restrained delivery without supplying the words.

Do not create a fresh speaker number inside an Audio definition. Speaker IDs are based
on the order of actual vocal events in the target video.


4.2 CLASSIFY THE JOB IN THE SUMMARY

The summary begins with one bracketed task declaration. Combine multiple roles inside
the same brackets with plus signs:

[reference generation]
[video continuation + keyframe completion]
[video editing + audio reuse]
[reference generation + audio reference]

Choose task roles by function rather than by file extension:

- keyframe completion: a picture must become a specific target frame.
- reference generation: an asset guides identity, environment, style, action, camera,
  storyboard logic, or sound without being directly copied or edited.
- video editing: the output is a modified version of an existing source video.
- video continuation: the output begins after, resumes, or extends a source video.
- audio reuse: all or part of an existing signal remains directly audible.
- audio reference: only properties such as voice, cadence, beat, musical language, or
  sound texture guide newly generated audio.

An input video used only for camera energy is reference generation, not video editing.
Likewise, an audio file used only for timbre is audio reference, not audio reuse. When a
source video is genuinely edited and its soundtrack remains, declare both video editing
and audio reuse. The summary may use only labels already introduced in
subject_definitions.


4.3 WRITE RETENTION ANALYSIS AS A TESTABLE CONTRACT

This section records what the output owes to every registered label. It should not
retell the plot. Include shot scope or frame role when useful, followed by exactly one
relationship marker and a concrete explanation.

Visual relationship markers:

- fully_preserved: all defining features or the entire declared role remain intact.
- partially_preserved: the same content remains identifiable, but declared features are
  intentionally altered or omitted.
- attribute_transfer: a selected property is applied to a different identifiable target.
- weak_reference: only a broad category, atmosphere, composition, or stylistic echo is
  requested.

Audio relationship markers:

- fully_copy: the complete supplied signal becomes the complete final soundtrack.
- partially_copy: only a time range or layer is reused, or copied sound is combined with
  newly generated material.
- reference: the signal guides specified audible properties without being copied.
- weak_reference: only a general sonic family or mood is borrowed.

Example entries:

<Subject 1> (appears in [Shot 1] and [Shot 2]): fully_preserved - facial anatomy, age,
silver pressure suit, and asymmetric haircut remain unchanged.
<Picture 2> ([Shot 2] final frame): fully_preserved - the final composition, camera
height, and relative subject placement are matched.
<Video 1> (camera acceleration): weak_reference - only the rise-and-settle rhythm is
echoed; its actors, location, palette, and choreography are excluded.
<Audio 1>: reference - vocal color and measured phrasing guide newly spoken dialogue;
the source waveform and original words are not carried over.

Judge retention only against the role declared in subject_definitions. New story action
is not a fidelity loss unless it changes something that the registry promised to keep.
Do not place (S1), (S2), or other speaker IDs in retention_analysis.


4.4 MAKE DETAILED DESCRIPTION A PLAYABLE TIMELINE

For a generation task, a useful detailed_description is often about 350 to 500 English
words. This is a working range, not a quota. Dialogue must fit the real duration, and a
simple direct edit may need much less prose. A one-shot clip still needs enough spatial,
lighting, movement, camera, and sound information to remove ambiguity.

Open with one or two sentences establishing the production language: realism or
stylization, lens behavior, palette, lighting logic, and editorial tone. Then write the
actual timeline:

- [Shot 1] has no timestamp.
- Every later cut starts with [Shot N] At MM:SS.mmm, ...
- Introduce a label when its referenced property becomes visible or audible.
- At first appearance, establish the subject's frame position, visible reference traits,
  state, and action.
- Cite a Picture naturally when a shot starts from, crosses, or ends on that frame.
- Cite a Video where editing, continuation, timing, or motion borrowing takes effect.
- Cite an Audio where its copied or referenced signal becomes active.
- Describe sound synchronized with a specific event inside the relevant shot.

Do not reduce this field to a plot synopsis or a list of reference mappings. The prose
must be playable: current composition, subject placement, light, action, state change,
camera behavior, audible events, and the timing of each reference contribution.


4.5 COMPLETE ORIGINAL REF2VA EXAMPLE

The following example uses a new setting and story. Its purpose is to demonstrate label
scope, task classification, retention, speaker binding, a motion reference, and a final
frame anchor without reproducing the subject matter of another guide.

subject_definitions:
<Subject 1> is the marine robotics engineer whose face, short black curls, copper-rimmed
glasses, teal coveralls, and compact build derive from <Picture 1>.
<Subject 2> is the damaged yellow survey drone shown in <Picture 3>, including its round
front camera, two folded manipulator arms, scratched shell, and blinking blue status bar.
<Picture 2> is the required final frame of [Shot 2], establishing <Subject 1> kneeling
beside <Subject 2>, the open pressure door behind them, and the green emergency light.
<Video 1> supplies only the camera's slow descending arc and its final two-second settle.
<Audio 1> is a voice-character reference for <Subject 1> (S1), guiding a low contralto
timbre, careful articulation, and quiet tension without copying the original recording.

summary:
[reference generation + keyframe completion + audio reference] The target is a
ten-second, two-shot recovery scene in an oceanographic pressure lab. <Subject 1>
retrieves <Subject 2> after a test-chamber alarm, reaches the composition fixed by
<Picture 2>, borrows only the camera cadence of <Video 1>, and speaks new dialogue using
the vocal character referenced from <Audio 1>.

retention_analysis:
<Subject 1> (appears in [Shot 1] and [Shot 2]): fully_preserved - facial structure,
short curls, copper-rimmed glasses, teal coveralls, body proportions, and apparent age
remain stable.
<Subject 2> (appears in [Shot 1] and [Shot 2]): fully_preserved - yellow scratched shell,
front camera, paired manipulator arms, and blue status bar remain recognizable.
<Picture 2> ([Shot 2] final frame): fully_preserved - final framing, engineer and drone
positions, open pressure door, and green emergency-light direction are matched.
<Video 1> (camera path in [Shot 2]): weak_reference - only its descending arc,
deceleration, and settling rhythm are echoed; source subjects and scenery are excluded.
<Audio 1>: reference - contralto timbre, articulation, breath spacing, and contained
tension guide new speech; neither the waveform nor its original words are copied.

detailed_description:
The target uses grounded near-future realism, cool steel surfaces, precise practical
lighting, and restrained camera movement. Condensation and warning lights provide color
without turning the lab into a glossy science-fiction set.
[Shot 1] A waist-high locked shot looks across an oceanographic pressure laboratory.
The circular chamber window occupies the left third of the frame; pipes, wet steel, and
a narrow drainage grate recede behind it. Red warning lamps pulse once per second.
<Subject 1>, preserving the short black curls, copper-rimmed glasses, teal coveralls,
compact proportions, and facial identity derived from <Picture 1>, braces both hands on
the chamber wheel. She turns it through half a rotation, shoulders tightening against
the resistance. The seal releases with a compressed metallic sigh. Cold vapor rolls
around her boots as she opens the heavy door just far enough to enter. Her glasses catch
one red pulse, but her eyes remain visible. The room alarm stops when the lock clears,
leaving pump vibration, escaping pressure, and water ticking onto the grate.
[Shot 2] At 00:04.200, the camera cuts inside the chamber to a medium-wide view. On the
wet floor, <Subject 2> retains its scratched yellow shell, round front camera, folded
manipulator arms, and blinking blue status bar from <Picture 3>. <Subject 1> steps into
frame, lowers into a controlled kneel, and checks the drone without changing its shape.
The camera adopts only the broad movement language of <Video 1>: it begins slightly
above shoulder height, descends through a slow clockwise arc, and reduces speed as she
turns the drone's front camera toward her. Bound to the vocal character of <Audio 1>,
<Subject 1> (S1) exhales and says, <d>[English] You found the current. Now show me where
it goes.</d> Her low contralto delivery is deliberate and tense; her lips articulate the
new line exactly, then close. The drone's blue bar changes from a broken blink to a
steady glow. During the final two seconds, green emergency light replaces the red pulse,
the camera becomes completely still, and subject motion decelerates into <Picture 2>:
the engineer kneeling beside the drone, the pressure door open behind them, and both
figures occupying the prescribed final positions. No extra cut and no new character.

overall_soundscape:
Low circulation pumps, periodic alarm pulses during [Shot 1], metal wheel strain,
pressure release, vapor, water on the drainage grate, coverall movement, a quiet drone
servo, and newly generated speech from <Subject 1>. The alarm ends before [Shot 2]; no
source audio from <Audio 1> is directly audible.

non_diegetic_music:
A sparse five-note pattern on muted glass percussion begins after the chamber opens. A
single low synthesized tone joins as the drone's status bar stabilizes, then both layers
resolve softly without a trailer-style impact.


5. DIALOGUE, VOICEOVER, LYRICS, AND AUDIO CONTROL

Assign (S1), (S2), and later IDs according to the order of real vocal events in the
target video. A referenced person keeps both identities when speaking: <Subject 2> (S1)
identifies the visible subject and the voice source. Reuse the same ID in every later
spoken or off-screen event from that speaker.

Put exact words inside <d> and identify their original language. Preserve wording and
language rather than translating or improving the line. If a referenced recording has
an unintelligible span that must be retained, use [unclear] instead of guessing. Basic
punctuation should make the performance readable; decorative symbols, emoji, and
repeated ornamental punctuation should not be treated as spoken content.

Good:

<Subject 1> (S1) looks at the mechanic and says,
<d>[Italian] Mi avevi detto che il motore avrebbe retto fino all'alba.</d>

Weak:

The man complains that the engine failed.

For voiceover, identify a stable off-screen narrator and state that visible mouths stay
closed unless lip synchronization is intended. When one sentence crosses an edit, use
<scenetrans> with a clear continuity instruction. When speech is deliberately cut by an
edit or by the end of the clip, use <cutoff>. Do not add these tokens to ordinary lines.

Distinguish two kinds of voice use:

- Direct reuse preserves the source words or waveform and must be declared as audio
  reuse with fully_copy or partially_copy.
- Voice reference generates a new performance with selected timbre, rhythm, or emotion;
  the original words must not leak into the new line.

If a sung or spoken phrase exists only inside a soundtrack that is being copied, cite
the relevant <Audio N> as its source. Do not invent (Sx) unless a concrete visible or
off-screen person, character, or narrator actually produces that voice in the target.

Visible text follows the same fidelity rule as dialogue. Keep the intended language and
write the exact sign, interface label, caption, or inscription in double quotes. Do not
translate it merely because the rest of the control prompt is English.

Keep localized sound synchronized with the visible event that causes it. Put that event
inside detailed_description. Use overall_soundscape for the continuous environment and
physical audio bed, not for repeating complete dialogue. Use non_diegetic_music only for
score that the characters cannot hear. Diegetic music, such as a radio playing inside
the room, belongs to the scene description and soundscape.

When one reference audio contains both ambience and audience-only music, describe each
relationship in its proper output section. State whether each layer is copied or merely
referenced; do not collapse all audio behavior into one vague sentence.


6. CAMERA AND EDITING LANGUAGE THAT PRODUCES BETTER RESULTS

Pair broad style with measurable behavior.

Instead of:
"A cinematic dynamic camera."

Use:
"A slow push-in from wide to medium over seven seconds, followed by a stable three-second
hold; no pan and no cut."

Instead of:
"The camera follows him dramatically."

Use:
"A shoulder-height handheld tracking shot follows two meters behind him, with restrained
vertical movement and one small lateral correction as he turns left."

Avoid stacking incompatible moves. A simultaneous static shot, orbit, zoom, and rapid
tracking instruction gives the model no clear priority. If the move changes, sequence it:
first track, then arc, then settle.

For cuts, make the transition explicit and timestamp every shot after Shot 1. Do not
timestamp Shot 1. In FL2VA, prefer no cuts unless the input images and creative goal
clearly require an edit rather than a continuous transition.


7. REFERENCE DISCIPLINE

More references do not automatically create more consistency. Every reference consumes
attention and can introduce competing composition, lighting, age, wardrobe, or motion
signals. Start with the minimum set that uniquely defines the goal.

Recommended hierarchy:

1. One strong identity image.
2. A second picture only if a different angle, costume detail, or final keyframe is
   genuinely required.
3. Video only when temporal motion, camera behavior, editing, or continuation is needed.
4. Audio only when voice, music, ambience, or exact sound reuse is needed.

For constrained hardware, use reference image sizing that matches the target canvas for
normal work. A maximum-size reference may improve small identity details in some cases,
but it also creates substantially more reference tokens and increases sampling cost.


8. LONG-FORM CONTINUITY WITH CHUNKS

H3 generates one conditioned clip at a time. Its shot syntax describes events inside
that clip; it is not a separate per-frame prompt-relay system. For a long sequence,
design several H3 generations and join them through visual and audio continuity.

A robust extension pattern is:

1. Generate chunk A.
2. Extract a clean final frame before any interpolation or upscale.
3. Use that frame as Picture 1 at 0.00 seconds for chunk B.
4. Keep a compact continuity block listing identity, clothing, weather, direction of
   movement, camera height, lens character, lighting, and unresolved action.
5. Write a new local action for chunk B instead of copying the complete prompt from A.
6. Carry room tone or music across the edit with a short audio crossfade.
7. Inspect the seam before running RIFE or upscale.

For stricter destination control, make chunk B an FL2VA generation: the previous final
frame becomes its opening frame and a planned storyboard image becomes its final frame.
The intermediate action remains local to that chunk.

Do not regenerate long dialogue independently in every chunk and expect phase-perfect
continuity. For a long spoken passage, create or retain a master speech track, divide it
at linguistic pauses, and use each segment as the corresponding audio condition when
the chosen REF2VA setup supports it. Preserve the same speaker definition across chunks
and use short audio crossfades at non-speech boundaries.


9. A PRACTICAL 10-SECOND PROMPT CHECKLIST

Before queueing a 243-frame, 24 fps generation, verify:

- The selected mode matches the loaded model and conditioning node.
- Picture labels match connection order.
- The first and final timestamps match the actual frame count.
- The prompt contains one achievable action arc.
- The camera path is physically continuous.
- Any cut after Shot 1 has a timestamp.
- Every speaking subject has one stable speaker identifier.
- Dialogue words appear once and are exact.
- Ambience, effects, and music are not duplicated.
- REF2VA retention roles say exactly what is preserved and what is only borrowed.
- There are no references or labels that are not connected.
- Technical output settings such as resolution, frame count, sampler, interpolation,
  and upscale remain in workflow controls, not in the creative prompt.


10. COMMON FAILURE PATTERNS AND REWRITES

Failure: The model ignores the last frame.
Rewrite: Reduce intermediate events, state the final time precisely, and reserve the
last second for deceleration and convergence to the target composition.

Failure: Character identity drifts during fast motion.
Rewrite: Simplify the fastest action, keep the face visible at a workable scale, define
the invariant identity once, and avoid contradictory face references.

Failure: Camera movement looks random.
Rewrite: Specify one primary move, direction, duration, magnitude, and ending behavior.

Failure: Audio becomes crowded.
Rewrite: Shorten dialogue, move ambience and effects to overall_soundscape, and remove
music unless it serves a clear purpose.

Failure: Reference video overwrites the intended character or location.
Rewrite: Mark motion and camera cadence as attribute_transfer while making reference
performer, wardrobe, set, and color weak_reference.

Failure: A ten-second prompt produces rushed storytelling.
Rewrite: Choose one scene objective, one main action, and one emotional change. Move the
next event into a second chunk.


11. FINAL WORKING PRINCIPLES

1. Prompt a timeline, not a poster.
2. Give every reference one explicit job.
3. In FL2VA, describe the path between images.
4. Prefer one coherent camera move over a list of cinematic terms.
5. Treat dialogue as exact production text.
6. Keep ambience, effects, and non-diegetic music separate.
7. Use timestamps for real cuts, not for every sentence.
8. Generate the simplest stable base clip before interpolation or upscale.
9. For long-form work, preserve continuity state between chunks while changing the local
   action prompt.
10. Test speed patches with the same prompt and seed because acceleration can change
    motion trajectories and fine detail.
